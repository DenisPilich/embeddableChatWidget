Why so much text

Without scrolling there is nothing to see, so the text below is long on purpose. It exists only to make the page scrollable.

Roasts come in light, medium and dark. A light roast keeps acidity and fruity notes but demands a finer grind setting: under-roasted is easy, over-roasted is easier. A dark roast gives bitterness and chocolate, forgives mistakes, and is therefore so popular in automatic machines.

Water matters more than the bean, tempting as it is to believe otherwise. Hardness, mineralisation and temperature affect extraction more than the country of origin: soft water opens up even an average bean, and nothing rescues a brew made with over-salted water.

Grind is the only variable you can change without buying anything new. Coarser grind — water passes faster, extraction is weaker, the cup tastes more sour. Finer grind — water lingers, extraction goes further, the cup turns bitter and dense.

Brewing temperature usually sits between 90 and 96 degrees. Below that the acidity is underdeveloped, above it you get bitterness and a risk of scorching the bean. Light roasts take a higher temperature, dark roasts a lower one, because a dark bean has already given up part of its flavour over the fire.

Ratio is a matter of habit, but start with sixty grams of coffee per litre of water. Adjust by taste afterwards: too strong — add water; too thin — grind finer rather than adding more coffee.

And finally, freshness. Two weeks after roasting the bean has noticeably lost aroma, and a month later no grind setting will save it. That is why we roast on Tuesdays and keep no stock.
